Corvette C7 vs. C8, Stingray to ZR1: A Real Buyer's Guide.

The C7 (2014–2019) was the last front-engine Corvette and the last one with a manual. The C8 (2020 on) moved the engine behind the driver and runs from a 490-hp Stingray to a 1,250-hp ZR1X. A used C7 Stingray now lists in the $30,000s, and a new C8 ZR1X starts at $227,395. Below: how the two generations differ, every trim from Stingray to ZR1 with what it sells for today, the known issues for each, and what to check before you buy.

C7 Vs. C8

The differences that actually matter.

  • Layout. The C7 is a traditional front-engine, rear-drive sports car. The C8 puts the engine behind the seats, which gives it far more traction off the line. Chevrolet rates a Z51 C8 Stingray at under 3 seconds to 60.
  • Transmission. Every C7 trim offered a 7-speed manual. Every C8 is a dual-clutch automatic. If you want three pedals in a modern Corvette, the C7 is your only option.
  • Price. A C7 Stingray is the cheapest way into a modern Corvette, and a C7 Grand Sport or Z06 costs about what a used C8 Stingray does. Early C8 Stingrays have dropped well below their original sticker; one 2021 3LT sold at auction for $57,250.
  • Everyday use. The C7 has a hatch over a usable rear cargo area. The C8 splits its storage between a front trunk and a rear trunk behind the engine.
  • Top of the range. The C7 tops out at the 755-hp ZR1, a one-year car. The C8 range goes much further: the 670-hp Z06, the hybrid all-wheel-drive E-Ray (replaced for 2027 by the Grand Sport X), and the 1,064-hp ZR1 and 1,250-hp ZR1X.
Trim By Trim

What each one is, and who it's for.

C7

  • Stingray (2014–2019): the entry point, 455 hp, or 460 hp with the performance exhaust. The Z51 package adds dry-sump oiling, bigger brakes, extra cooling and an electronic limited-slip differential. Early automatic cars start around $30,000; the 2014 used a 6-speed automatic, and 2015 on got the 8-speed.
  • Grand Sport (2017–2019): the Stingray's engine in the Z06's wide body, with its brakes, cooling and suspension tuning. It cost $66,445 new in 2017. 26,280 were built, about 21% of them manuals, and 2,028 had the Z07 package (carbon-ceramic brakes, Michelin Pilot Sport Cup 2 tires). Many people think it's the best-balanced C7.
  • Z06 (2015–2019): supercharged, 650 hp and 650 lb-ft. It's the fastest C7 short of the ZR1, but early cars are known for overheating on the track (see below).
  • ZR1 (2019): the supercharged 755-hp LT5, the most powerful Chevrolet ever built when it launched. It was a one-year car: 2,953 built, 2,441 coupes and 512 convertibles, with only 744 manual coupes. It holds its value better than any other C7.

C8

  • Stingray (2020 on): 490 hp, or 495 hp with the Z51 package. Z51 cars hold their value noticeably better than non-Z51 cars. For 2027 the Stingray moves to the new 6.7L LS6 with 535 hp and starts at $73,495.
  • E-Ray (2024–2026): the Stingray's V8 plus a 160-hp electric motor driving the front wheels, 655 hp total with all-wheel drive and a 2.5-second 0–60. Used E-Rays list close to or below their original sticker.
  • Grand Sport and Grand Sport X (2027): the name returns on the C8. The Grand Sport uses the 535-hp LS6. The Grand Sport X adds a front motor for 721 hp and all-wheel drive, and replaces the E-Ray.
  • Z06 (2023 on): a 5.5L flat-plane-crank V8 that revs past 8,000 rpm, 670 hp, 0–60 in 2.6 seconds. Dealer markups have shrunk a lot since launch, and used Z06s now list around new-car money.
  • ZR1 (2025 on) and ZR1X (2026 on): a twin-turbo version of the Z06's engine making 1,064 hp, and a hybrid all-wheel-drive version making 1,250 hp. ZR1s are listed roughly $90,000–$145,000 over MSRP, so a markup is still the norm at this level.
Is It Reliable?

Known issues by generation.

Corvette V8s have a strong reputation, and none of the issues below are engine failures. Most are specific and well documented, so a seller should be able to show whether each one has been dealt with.

C7

  • Bent and cracked wheels (Grand Sport and Z06). These two share wide cast wheels that owners say bend and crack under normal driving. Car and Driver's 40,000-mile long-term Grand Sport needed seven wheel repairs and three replacements, $4,098 in total. Several class actions followed, and GM set up a special reimbursement program for wheel damage during the warranty and extended coverage period. The catch is that the owner has to show the damage wasn't caused by a road hazard.
  • 8-speed automatic shudder (2015–2019). The 8L90 can develop a torque-converter shudder. GM's bulletin (TSB 18-NA-355) calls for flushing the transmission with updated Mobil 1 LV ATF HP fluid, and that usually fixes it. Early cars are the most affected.
  • Z06 overheating on track (mainly 2015–2016). Early Z06s, especially automatics, could drop into limp mode on hot track days. GM revised the cooling for 2017 and offered upgraded radiators and updated shift calibrations under special coverage on 2015–2016 manuals and 2015–2018 automatics. That coverage ran 7 years or 72,000 miles from the in-service date, so most cars have aged out of it. Ask whether the update was done.

C8

  • Early-2020 valve springs. Some early cars got replacement valve springs under warranty as a service update, not a recall. Check the records on any 2020 car.
  • Recalls. Recalls have covered rear half shafts on some 2021–2022 cars, seat belt retractors (the first batch of replacements had the same defect), the front trunk release and latch, and a fuel-filler fire risk on 2023–2026 Z06 and 2025–2026 ZR1 cars (25V-536). Recent Stingray, E-Ray and ZR1X cars also have a turn-signal software recall.
  • Smaller complaints. Some 2020 cars drained their 12-volt battery while parked, and water leaks into the front and rear trunks are a common owner complaint.

On any Corvette, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and have a Chevrolet dealer check for open campaigns and bulletins.

Ownership Costs

What to budget for.

  • Oil changes: dry-sump cars (C7 Z51 Stingray, Grand Sport, Z06 and ZR1, and every C8) hold far more oil than a typical V8. The C7 Grand Sport takes about 10 quarts of dexos2-approved oil (GM specifies Mobil 1 0W-40). The oil life monitor sets the interval.
  • Tires: C7 Stingrays and Grand Sports came on run-flats that have been listed at roughly $460–$650 per tire depending on size, so a full set runs about $2,000 or more installed. Z07 and Z06/ZR1 cars on Cup 2-type tires wear through them faster.
  • Brakes: carbon-ceramic brakes (C7 Z07 package, and common on the C8 Z06 and ZR1) last a long time on the street, but replacement rotors are very expensive. Check rotor condition before you buy.
  • Wheels: on a C7 Grand Sport or Z06 that sees rough roads, set money aside for wheel straightening or replacement.
  • New-car extras: the federal gas-guzzler tax adds $2,600–$3,000 to a new Z06, ZR1 or ZR1X.

Tire prices are from older retail listings and will have moved. Get a current quote.

Before You Buy

What to look for.

  • Recalls and bulletins. Run the VIN and ask for proof that open campaigns were done, especially the seat belt and fuel-filler recalls on a C8 and the transmission flush on a C7 automatic.
  • Wheels on a C7 Grand Sport or Z06. Have each wheel checked on a balancer for bends and look at the inner barrels for cracks. Ask for receipts for any wheel repairs.
  • Track use. Look at brake pad and rotor wear, tire wear patterns, and any aftermarket brake fluid or pads. On a C7 Z06, ask whether the cooling update was done.
  • The right packages. On a C8 Stingray, Z51 cars hold their value better. On a C7, confirm the Z51, Z07 or special-edition equipment against the RPO codes on the build sheet.
  • Roof and top. On coupes, check the removable roof panel's seals and latches for leaks and rattles. On convertibles, run the top through a full cycle. On a C8, also check both trunks for water.
  • New C8 Z06, ZR1 or ZR1X pricing. Know the MSRP and what comparable cars are actually listed at before you accept a markup.
Questions

Straight answers.

C7 or C8 — which should I buy?

If you want a manual or a traditional front-engine car, or the cheapest way into a modern Corvette, buy a C7. If you want the quickest car for the money and the newest tech, buy a C8. A used C7 Grand Sport or Z06 and an early C8 Stingray cost about the same.

What's the cheapest way into a modern Corvette?

A C7 Stingray. Early automatic cars list from around $30,000, and most 2014–2019 Stingrays list between $30,000 and $55,000 depending on year, trim, mileage and transmission.

Which Corvettes hold their value best?

Limited, top-of-range cars. The one-year 2019 C7 ZR1 (2,953 built) still lists from about $174,000, and the C8 ZR1 is listed well over its MSRP. Manual C7s, especially Grand Sports and Z06s, are scarcer than automatics. On a C8 Stingray, Z51 cars hold up better than non-Z51 cars.

Do C7 Corvette wheels crack?

On the Grand Sport and Z06, it's the best-documented issue. They share wide cast wheels that owners report bending and cracking, and the problem led to class actions and a GM reimbursement program for damage during warranty coverage. Have every wheel checked on a balancer before you buy.

Are dealers still marking up the C8?

Mostly on the ZR1, which is listed roughly $90,000–$145,000 over MSRP. Z06 markups have largely faded, and used Z06s and E-Rays now list around or below what they cost new. Compare against current listings before you agree to any markup.
