The Audi R8 Buying Guide: Every Trim, Explained.

Every R8 shares the same mid-engine layout and Lamborghini-derived platform, but "R8" actually spans two full generations and several distinct trims that drive differently and hold value differently. Short version: V10s are quicker and more sought-after than the V8, manual cars — Gen 1 only, 2008–2015 — carry a real premium over any automatic, and the V10 Plus / V10 Performance halo trim has generally held its value best because Audi simply built far fewer of them. Below is a trim-by-trim breakdown, what actually drives the price gaps between them, and what to check before you buy one.

Trim By Trim
Gen 1 Only · 2008–2015

V8 & V8 Spyder

ENGINE 4.2L V8 POWER 420–430 hp 0–60 ~4.5–4.7s TRANS 6MT / R tronic / S tronic

What It Is

The entry point into R8 ownership, and the only naturally aspirated V8 R8 Audi ever built — the second generation dropped the V8 entirely and went V10-only. Early cars (2008–2012) paired the 4.2 FSI V8 with a 6-speed manual or Audi's single-clutch "R tronic" automated gearbox, which is a genuinely different and clunkier thing than a modern dual-clutch. The 2013 facelift bumped power slightly and swapped R tronic for a proper 7-speed S tronic dual-clutch, alongside the manual. The Spyder (convertible) followed the coupe roughly a model year behind.

Pricing Trend

This is the most common R8 on the road, and it prices accordingly — it's the softest, most accessible entry point into the model. It also depreciated the most predictably of any R8 trim, since it never carried the V10's exclusivity or soundtrack. That said, well-kept, lower-mileage examples — especially manuals — have shown real staying power rather than continuing to slide, which tracks with how the broader exotic market has treated clean Gen 1 R8s generally.

Value Drivers

Manual transmission is the single biggest swing factor within this trim — a stick V8 draws a different, more dedicated buyer than an R tronic or S tronic car. R tronic cars specifically tend to price lower than S tronic facelift cars, partly because the gearbox itself has a mixed reputation and partly because buyers now associate "modern R8 automatic" with S tronic, not R tronic. Mileage and service history matter more here than on the V10s, since this is the trim most likely to have been bought as a "cheap exotic" and driven hard without the maintenance budget to match.

Gen 1 & Gen 2 · 2009–2023

V10 & V10 Spyder

ENGINE 5.2L V10 POWER 525–540 hp 0–60 ~3.5–4.2s TRANS 6MT / R tronic (Gen 1) · S tronic only (Gen 2)

What It Is

The R8 most enthusiasts mean when they say "R8." Same basic architecture as the V8, longer and bored out to ten cylinders, with a noticeably different soundtrack and a real step up in outright pace. In Gen 1 (2009–2015) it was offered manual, R tronic, or — after the 2013 facelift — S tronic, mirroring the V8's transmission history. Gen 2 (2017–2023) is a different animal entirely: S tronic dual-clutch only, no manual was ever offered, quattro all-wheel drive standard, and meaningfully quicker across the board than any Gen 1 car.

Pricing Trend

Sits a clear step above the V8 in both directions — higher new price historically and a firmer used-market floor. Gen 1 manual V10s in particular have become the trim enthusiasts specifically search for, and that demand has kept them from sliding the way a typical 15-year-old exotic would. Gen 2 S tronic cars trade more like a modern performance car — steadier depreciation curve, less of the "collector premium" that's built up around the manual Gen 1 cars, since a plentiful, all-automatic used pool keeps pricing more competitive.

Value Drivers

Generation is the first fork in the road — Gen 1 for the option of a manual and a more analog feel, Gen 2 for outright performance and a modern cabin. Within Gen 1, manual vs. R tronic vs. S tronic creates three distinct price tiers, manual highest. Spyder vs. coupe is mostly a personal-preference split rather than a clear value hierarchy, though coupes are generally viewed as the "purer" driver's choice by the enthusiast crowd that drives V10 demand.

Gen 1 & Gen 2 Halo Trim

V10 Plus / V10 Performance

ENGINE 5.2L V10 POWER 550–620 hp 0–60 low-3s to high-2s TRANS 6MT (Gen 1) / S tronic only (Gen 2)

What It Is

The R8's track-focused top trim — worth clearing up the name first. Audi has called it "V10 plus" (Gen 1, and early Gen 2) and later "V10 performance" (later Gen 2); it's never officially been called "Performante" — that's Lamborghini's name for the equivalent Huracán, a common mix-up given the shared platform. Whatever the badge says, this is the version with the most power, the most aggressive aero, and the widest performance gap over the standard V10. Late in Gen 2, Audi also offered a rear-wheel-drive-only version of this trim in genuinely limited numbers — the sharpest, most stripped-down R8 ever built.

Pricing Trend

This is the trim that has held its value most firmly of the entire lineup, in both generations. Production numbers were always low relative to the standard V10, and buyers who specifically seek out the halo trim tend to be collectors and enthusiasts rather than people cross-shopping generic exotics — that combination has kept the used market for these noticeably tighter than for standard V10s. The RWD-only late-Gen-2 cars sit in a category of their own: genuinely scarce, and priced like it.

Value Drivers

Rarity is the whole story here — this is the lowest-production R8 trim in either generation, and it shows in how it's traded. A manual Gen 1 V10 plus is about as sought-after as an R8 gets. On Gen 2, the RWD variant commands its own premium over the standard quattro V10 performance, purely on scarcity and the fact that it's the closest thing Audi ever built to a driver-first R8.

What Actually Drives Value

It's rarely just the trim badge.

Rarity. Classic.com's data shows this directly: the GT (333 built, 2012 only) averages $118,940 and the Competition (2015 only, limited production) averages $128,722 — both ahead of the more common V10 Plus at $114,281, despite none of them being a dramatic performance step up over one another. That gap is production scarcity showing up in price, not raw specs.

Manual transmission. Only ever offered on Gen 1 (2008–2015), on both V8 and V10. Since Audi went S-tronic-only for Gen 2, the pool of manual R8s can only shrink, and demand for them has grown accordingly — it's the clearest, most consistent value driver across the entire model. (Classic.com's published averages blend transmission types together, so this is a directional read rather than a broken-out manual-vs-automatic figure.)

Mileage bands. Like most exotics, R8 values step down in bands rather than sliding evenly — sub-20k, 20–50k, and 50k+ tend to be where buyers mentally reset their offer, more than the mileage number itself moving in a straight line. A car just under a band threshold is usually worth disproportionately more than one just over it.

Generation. The step from Type 42 to Type 4S isn't subtle in the data — Type 42's overall average sits at $97,256 against Type 4S's $150,972, a step-up of roughly 55%, and the Type 4S V10 Performance RWD Coupe averages $163,388, the highest figure in the lineup. Gen 1 vs. Gen 2 isn't just an age difference — it's a fork between "manual, more analog, cheaper to enter" and "quicker, modern, S tronic only," and the market is clearly pricing that fork.

Before You Buy

What to look for.

  • A pre-purchase inspection from an Audi/VW specialist — not a generic mechanic, and not the selling dealer. R8-specific knowledge matters here.
  • Full service history, especially on higher-mileage cars — these are expensive to catch up on if maintenance was deferred, and a documented history is worth real money at resale too.
  • Clutch condition on manual cars — have it assessed directly rather than trusting mileage alone as a proxy for wear.
  • Convertible top operation and seals on any Spyder — cycle it in person, check for wear or leaks around the mechanism.
  • Tire and brake wear — R8s wear expensive, exotic-width tires and offer optional carbon-ceramic brakes; both are costly to replace and worth pricing into your offer.
  • Modification and track-use history — ask directly. A well-documented, mechanically sound modified car isn't automatically a red flag, but an undisclosed one is.
Questions

Straight answers.

Which R8 trim holds value best?

The V10 Plus / V10 Performance halo trim has generally held its value best, simply because Audi built far fewer of them than the standard V8 and V10. Manual examples of any R8 add a further layer of value retention on top of that, since manual was only ever offered on Gen 1 cars and demand for it has only grown since Audi dropped it. The base V8 is the softest holder of value — it's the most common R8 on the road and the one enthusiasts chase last.

Is the manual worth the premium?

For resale and collector value, yes — manual R8s (Gen 1 only, 2008–2015) are scarcer than the S tronic and R tronic cars that make up most of the used market, and that scarcity has only become more pronounced since Audi went S-tronic-only for Gen 2. For everyday usability it's a trade-off: you give up some smoothness in traffic and some resale liquidity, since the buyer pool for a 3-pedal exotic is smaller. If you're buying to drive and eventually sell, the manual premium has generally been worth paying; if you just want the easiest ownership experience, S tronic is the more practical choice.

V8 vs. V10 — what's the real difference?

More than just horsepower. The V10 is the same basic Lamborghini-derived architecture as the V8 but bored out, and it brings a noticeably different soundtrack, a real step up in outright performance, and more prestige within the R8 lineup — most enthusiasts consider the V10 the "real" R8. That comes with higher running costs (tires, brakes, insurance) and a higher purchase price. The V8 is the more attainable, still-quick way into R8 ownership — and it's Gen 1 only, since Audi never brought it back for the second generation.

Gen 1 or Gen 2 — which should I buy?

It depends what you're optimizing for. Gen 1 (2008–2015) is the only place to find a manual, has a more analog driving character, and is generally the cheaper way into ownership. Gen 2 (2017–2023) is quicker across the board, with a sharper chassis and modern interior tech, but it's S tronic only — there's no manual Gen 2 R8. If the manual gearbox matters to you, that decision is effectively made for you.
