The Lamborghini Huracán: A Real Buyer's Guide.

The Huracán is the last naturally aspirated V10 Lamborghini, and the early LP610-4 and LP580-2 cars now trade in the high-$100,000s to low-$200,000s. That's well under what the later EVO and STO bring. Below is what matters before you buy one: the specs, where the market actually is, the recalls and known issues, what it costs to keep one on the road, and what to check on a specific car.

Specs At A Glance

The numbers that matter.

ENGINE 5.2L naturally aspirated V10 POWER 602 hp (LP610-4) TORQUE 413 lb-ft 0–62 3.2s TOP SPEED 202 mph
  • Transmission: 7-speed LDF dual-clutch on every Huracán. No manual was offered.
  • LP610-4: the original all-wheel-drive car, introduced at Geneva in 2014 as the Gallardo's replacement. US model years run 2015–2019. The name carries the output: 610 PS, about 602 hp.
  • LP580-2: the rear-wheel-drive car, from the 2016 model year. 580 PS, about 572 hp, and less weight over the nose.
  • Spyder: offered from the 2016 model year in both drivetrains.
  • Later variants: Performante, EVO (631 hp, AWD), EVO RWD, STO, Tecnica and Sterrato. These are priced in a different bracket, covered below.

Power figures are the manufacturer's PS ratings converted to hp. 0–62 and top speed are Lamborghini's LP610-4 coupe claims; published road tests vary.

Why It Makes Sense Right Now

The end of the V10 era.

The Huracán's replacement, the Temerario, is a plug-in hybrid with a twin-turbo V8. That makes the Huracán the last V10 Lamborghini, and the early cars are the most affordable way into one.

The gap between early and late cars is wide. Classic.com puts the LP610-4 benchmark around $177,000, the EVO around $249,000–$267,000, and the STO around $366,000. The early cars share the same V10, the same dual-clutch and the same basic chassis as the later ones. What you give up is the EVO's rear-wheel steering and torque vectoring and its newer infotainment.

One thing works in a buyer's favor: dealer asking prices on early cars sit above where they actually sell. Many LP610-4 coupes are listed in the $190,000s to $260,000s, while auction sales tracked across Bring a Trailer, Cars & Bids, PCarMarket and others average around $200,000. There's usually room to negotiate.

Across 679 tracked LP610-4 auction sales, prices ran from about $141,700 to $285,000, averaging around $200,000. That range mixes years, mileage and condition. Mileage, Spyder vs. coupe, and options move individual cars a long way.

Is It Reliable?

Recalls and known issues.

The Huracán's V10 and dual-clutch are shared with the second-generation Audi R8, and the drivetrain has a solid reputation. Owner reports point to a handful of specific problems, not a pattern of major failures:

  • Battery drain. The car's electronics draw a constant load and can flatten the battery in a week or two of sitting. A battery tender is standard equipment for owners.
  • Front lift system. Hydraulic pump or seal failures show up as a lift fault warning and a nose that won't raise. This is reported mostly on later cars, but check it on any car that has the option.
  • Oil leaks at the rear lower timing cover, reported on some 2015–2018 cars. Look for it on a lift: the repair is engine-out and expensive.
  • Clutch wear. The LDF dual-clutch is generally durable, but cars that were launched often or tracked hard can show slip early. A dealer scan can read clutch wear.

There are three NHTSA recalls that touch early cars. All are free fixes at a Lamborghini dealer:

  • 22V-081 (2015–2020): missing caps on the headlight adjustment screws.
  • 23V-826 (2015–2024): headlight aim. A software update disables Tourist Mode and the adaptive front lighting.
  • 21V-466 (2018–2021): rearview camera may not display below about 41°F. Software update.

Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and ask a Lamborghini dealer for the car's open campaigns before you buy.

Ownership Costs

What to budget for.

  • Annual service: the factory schedule is yearly (roughly every 9,000–10,000 miles). Published estimates run from about $850–$1,500 at an independent specialist to well over that at a dealer.
  • Tires: about $3,000–$5,000 a set.
  • Brakes: about $2,000–$4,000 for pads and rotors on steel brakes. Carbon-ceramic rotors last much longer but cost far more to replace.
  • All in: owners commonly budget $1,500–$4,000 a year in routine running costs before anything unexpected.

Warranty. Lamborghini's Selezione certified pre-owned program carries a 12- to 24-month factory warranty that transfers to later owners, with roadside assistance for the same period. Eligibility has an age and mileage cutoff (Lamborghini lists 84 months and 70,000 km for its super sports cars), so many original LP610-4 and LP580-2 cars are now at or past the limit. Check eligibility car by car. Where a car doesn't qualify, an independent pre-purchase inspection matters even more.

Cost figures are published estimates from owner and specialist sources and vary by shop and region. Get a quote from the shop that will service the car.

Before You Buy

What to look for.

  • LP610-4 or LP580-2. All-wheel drive gives more traction and is the easier car to live with. Rear-wheel drive is lighter at the front and more playful. The two trade in the same price range, so year, mileage and condition move the price more than drivetrain does.
  • Front lift. It's close to essential on a car this low if you'll use driveways, ramps or speed bumps. Confirm the car has it and that it works.
  • Service records. Look for annual services on time with documented fluid changes, not just oil.
  • Clutch and DCT health. Ask for a dealer scan of clutch wear, and ask how the car was used (track days, launches).
  • Underside. Check for timing-cover oil seepage and for scrape damage to the front splitter and underbody.
  • Recalls. Confirm all three NHTSA campaigns above are closed.
Questions

Straight answers.

How much is a used Lamborghini Huracán?

Early LP610-4 cars (2015–2019) have a Classic.com benchmark around $177,000, and tracked auction sales average about $200,000. Dealer asking prices commonly run $190,000–$270,000. EVOs sit around $249,000–$267,000 and STOs around $366,000.

Is the Huracán reliable?

The V10 and dual-clutch drivetrain has a solid reputation. Known issues are specific: battery drain when the car sits, front lift pump failures, timing-cover oil leaks on some early cars, and clutch wear on hard-driven cars. There are three NHTSA recalls covering early cars, all free fixes at a dealer.

What does a Huracán cost to maintain?

Annual service runs roughly $850–$1,500 at an independent specialist and more at a dealer. Tires are about $3,000–$5,000 a set and steel-brake pads and rotors about $2,000–$4,000. Owners commonly budget $1,500–$4,000 a year before anything unexpected.

Can I get a certified pre-owned Huracán?

Lamborghini's Selezione program offers a 12- to 24-month transferable warranty, but it has age and mileage limits, so many early LP610-4 and LP580-2 cars no longer qualify. Ask the dealer whether a specific car is eligible, and get an independent pre-purchase inspection either way.
